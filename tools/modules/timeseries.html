Demography and population dynamics

Population time series - trends, density dependence and drivers of growth (state-space models)

"Is my population declining, how fast, and what drives good and bad years?" Annual counts bounce for two different reasons: the population really changes (births, deaths, a hard winter: process noise) and the count misses or double-counts animals (observation error). State-space models separate the two. That matters because

module id: timeseries! Example verdict: OPEN5 of 8 quantities certified

Is this the right analysis?

Use it when

One count or abundance index per year (aerial/ground counts, nest counts, redd counts, CPUE, BBS-style route totals) for one population or several sub-populations, 10+ years; the question is whether the population is increasing or declining and by how much per year, how large it really is once counting error is removed, whether growth is density dependent, whether a covariate (winter severity, harvest, drought) is associated with good and bad years, and how many years of monitoring are needed to detect a decline.

Not for

Repeated counts within a season at many sites (use nmixture, which estimates detection); marked animals (cjs / jolly-seber / ipm give vital rates); detection/non-detection (occu-*); age- or stage-structured projections and extinction risk (pva); series shorter than ~8 years (no model can separate process from observation error).

The data you need

CSV with one row per year (per site): year, count or index, optional site/herd column, optional yearly covariates; years not surveyed may be blank or absent.

Try it

The worked example

Is a mule deer herd declining, and do deep-snow winters reduce its growth? (Bayesian state-space model, simulated data)

The question: Is the herd declining and by how much per year, how many deer are really there once counting error is removed, is growth density dependent, and do deep-snow winters reduce herd growth?

The data are simulated, so the true answer is known and the analysis can be checked against it.

What the researcher typed

I've attached herd_counts.csv: aerial winter counts of our mule deer herd, 1994-2024, with mean winter snow depth from the snow course. Three winters weren't flown. Please tell me whether the herd is declining and by how much per year, how many deer there really are, whether growth is density dependent, and whether deep-snow winters reduce herd growth. I don't code, so please give me a report with figures I can use in a paper.

What the report says

Results from the example data

What this means

  • The estimated true population changed by +0.1% per year on average between 1994 and 2024 (95% credible interval -1.4% to +1.3%).
  • Estimated true abundance (on the scale of the animals counted) in 2024: 614 (95% credible interval 499 to 768). If counts miss a constant fraction of animals this is an index of abundance, not a census.
  • Equilibrium abundance (where growth averages zero, at average covariates): 536 (95% credible interval 478 to 599).
  • An increase of one standard deviation (20) in Mean winter snow depth (cm) is associated with a -15.3% change in the annual growth multiplier (95% credible interval -22.2% to -7.0%; P(effect > 0) = 0.00). At average abundance the population is expected to change by +19.9% in a year with snow depth at its 10th percentile (30.9 cm) vs -16.4% at its 90th percentile (74.5 cm). This is an association, not proof that snow depth causes the change.
  • Counting error (observation SD 0.18, 95% CI 0.03-0.28) is estimated to be larger than real year-to-year variation (process SD 0.10, 0.00-0.26), so single year-to-year changes in raw counts should not be read as real population changes.
  • Because the prior allows only b between -2 and 0, the credible interval for b can never include 0, so it is not evidence that density dependence exists; b from a single count series is overestimated (here -0.59 vs true -0.30).
  • Posterior predictive check: the variability (Bayesian p = 0.61) and lag-1 autocorrelation (p = 0.75) of year-to-year changes match the observed counts, so there is no sign of misfit.
  • Formal test for density dependence (Gompertz parametric-bootstrap likelihood ratio, 199 simulated series): p = 0.585; the counts are consistent with density-independent growth, but this test has little power.

Limits that matter

  • Density dependence is not recoverable from a single count series (Knape 2008): b biased -59% at this design; in bayes the U(-2,0) prior means its interval never contains 0.
  • Process and observation SDs are biased (+23% and -13% here); trend and abundance intervals remain valid.
  • Constant detection is assumed: herd size is an index on the count scale, and a change in sightability would look like a trend.
  • 'No clear trend' is weak evidence: a single series declining 5%/yr is detected with power 0.29 after 20 years and 0.49 after 30.
  • Bayes density-dependence fits can mix slowly: 7% of fast-setting replicates missed the convergence rule; the real fit (4 x 40,000) had min ESS 684.
  • Covariate effects are associations, not causes.
  • The Gompertz bootstrap test of density dependence holds its false-positive rate but has 2-6% power at b = -0.3.
All known limits from the module's validation record
  1. Variance components from one series. sd_process is biased (REML -17% at 25 yr; Bayes +31%) and sd_observation is biased in the Gompertz and Bayes models. Trend and abundance intervals stay valid. Pooling sites (marss) or an independent estimate of counting error (prior_sd_observation) fixes it.

  2. Density dependence is not recoverable from a single count series (Knape 2008; Auger-Methe et al. 2016). Point estimates of b are overestimated by 30-140% depending on length and engine. The profile interval for b excludes 0 in 47% of series without density dependence.

    • The bootstrap LRT holds its size (0.04-0.07) but has almost no power at 15 years.
    • In bayes the U(-2, 0) prior means the interval for b never contains 0.
    • The interpretation text states all of this.
  3. equilibrium_N (Gompertz ML) is undefined when b is near 0. The mean of the MLEs is dominated by huge values and coverage is 0.889 at 30 yr. The report suppresses K when its interval spans more than a 20-fold range.
  4. Trend intervals are conservative. The false-positive rate is 0.025, and coverage is 0.97-1.0 below 20 years. Power is therefore lower than with plug-in intervals, which are invalid (coverage 0.81).
  5. process_cov: equalvarcov (MARSS EM) converged in only 75% of replicates. Treat process_corr as exploratory.
  6. loglinear with 3 sites x 10 years: 14.5% of fits have a singular Hessian. It also does not separate process from observation error.
  7. Constant detection is assumed. Abundance is on the count scale, and a trend in detectability is indistinguishable from a population trend.
  8. The importance sampler for ML abundance intervals can have low ESS (<200) for Gompertz fits with a strongly curved likelihood. The fit then adds a note.
  9. Bayes DD fits mix slowly when b is poorly identified. In the 60-year cross-check, minimum ESS was 188 at 4 x 40,000 iterations; the report flags runs below ESS 400.
  1. REML trend intervals are very conservative when counting error dominates. At 20 years with sd_process 0.06 and sd_observation 0.25, a +4%/yr trend was never detected (power 0.000, false-positive rate 0.000, coverage 1.000), whereas GLS with the true variances would detect it in 60% of series. The intervals are valid but can be about 3x wider than necessary; see "Independent verification". The report now says that "no clear trend" is not evidence of stability and points to a power analysis.
  2. loglinear trend intervals are too narrow when the population itself wanders (random walk with drift plus counting error): coverage 0.825 at 4 sites x 25 years. The report warns when the fitted AR(1) correlation exceeds 0.9 and always says to compare with marss/exponential.

How this module was validated

  • 100-200 simulated datasets per design across five engines: single-series REML trend, multi-site MARSS, Gompertz maximum likelihood, log-linear GLMM and Bayesian state-space. The Bayesian fits used 3 chains x 12,000 iterations, 4,000 burn-in. To pass, bias had to be within 10% (or 3 Monte Carlo SE) and coverage (how often the 95% interval contained the truth) at least 0.885-0.904.
  • Passed: trends, final abundance, covariate effects on growth and equilibrium abundance. Failed: density dependence at the default noise levels (Gompertz ML -96% at 30 years, Bayes -62% at 25 years) and process SD from a single series (REML -17%).
  • Harder designs: 12-year series, 30% missing years and multi-site series with gaps all kept trend coverage. The log-linear model with 3 sites x 10 years failed on convergence (85.5% converged).
  • Negative control: ignoring counting error made density dependence look 2.6 times stronger (coverage 0.275) and dropped final-abundance coverage to 0.000.
  • False-positive rates: 0.025 for the REML trend, 0.045 for MARSS and 0.031 for the Bayesian covariate. The Gompertz profile interval for b excluded 0 in 47% of series with no density dependence, so the bootstrap test (0.04-0.07) is used instead.
  • Cross-checks: the module matched the MARSS package (log-likelihood within 6e-4) and MASS glm.nb (within 1e-6), and the Bayesian fits agreed with Kalman maximum likelihood on long series.
  • Power: a 5%-per-year decline in one population reached 0.71 power only at 40 years, while 4 sites over 25 years gave 0.735 for a 3% decline. A separate verifier agent found that b can be recovered (Gompertz ML at 40 years, b = -0.5, process noise larger than counting error), but the Bayesian model still overstated it by 33%.

Every run uses the acceptance gate described on the validation page. The full record follows, section by section, exactly as kept in the module's VALIDATION.md.

Bottom line for researchers:

  • Trends, the trajectory of true abundance and covariate effects on growth are recovered with honest intervals at realistic designs.
  • Process and observation SDs from one series of 25 years are biased by about 15-40%. Do not over-interpret them.
  • Density dependence from one count series is not recoverable without independent information on counting error. Even with it, b is overestimated (bayes_obsprior: -52%).

Why REML is the default:

  • Plain ML estimates sd_process at exactly 0 in many series, and the trend interval then collapses (coverage 0.675).
  • REML integrates the variances out on a grid (flat prior on both SDs), which keeps trend intervals honest.
  • The price is conservatism: coverage is 0.97-1.0 below 20 years, and the false-positive rate is 0.025 instead of 0.05 (section 4).

  • dev/timeseries/prior_study.R (results/timeseries/prior_study.csv) compared four ways of handling the variances at 25 years (sd_p 0.1, sd_o 0.15). Each pair is coverage / power:

    • plug-in GLS: 0.81 / 0.42 (invalid);
    • log-uniform prior: 0.91 / 0.28;
    • sqrt prior: 0.95 / 0.20;
    • flat prior: 0.968 / 0.165.
  • Flat was kept because it is the only option with coverage >= 0.90 in every scenario studied.

sd_process (single series) is the one default-design estimand that fails:

  • REML puts it on the boundary (0) in about 14% of 25-year series.
  • dev/timeseries/sd_estimator_study.R tried the grid-posterior median (+12%) and mean (+20%). No point estimator came within 10% at 25 years. At 50 years REML bias is -6.6%.
  • Interval coverage is fine (0.98). The point estimate is not reliable, and the interpretation text says so when it is at the boundary.

Fix made in this round (ML abundance intervals). Abundance intervals for the ML engines previously drew parameters from the asymptotic normal (Hessian) distribution. That collapsed to zero width whenever sd_observation was estimated at 0, and exploded when b was drawn near -2. The coverage of Gompertz N_final was 0.573. They now use adaptive sampling-importance- resampling from the likelihood (kalman.R kf_sir):

  • flat priors, with the SDs flat on the SD scale;
  • a multivariate-t proposal, re-centred over 3 pilot rounds;
  • typical importance ESS of 1,300-4,400 for exponential and 200-1,300 for Gompertz, out of 20,000 proposals; the fit notes when ESS < 200.

After the change, N_final coverage is 0.920 (Gompertz, 30 yr) and 0.960 (exponential ML, previously 0.79).

How to use these results:

  • Test density dependence with the bootstrap LRT, never with the interval for b.
  • With realistic noise (sd_p 0.1, sd_o 0.15) the test has essentially no power at 15 or even 30 years (rejection rate at b = -0.3 is no higher than at b = 0), as Knape (2008) predicts. A non-significant test therefore says nothing about whether density dependence is absent.

What this means for managers:

  • With real year-to-year variation of 10% (sd_process 0.1), a single monitored population declining 5% a year is detected with 80% power only after more than 40 years.
  • The drift of a random walk is inherently hard to estimate, and the module's integrated intervals are deliberately conservative.
  • Monitoring several sub-populations with a shared trend helps a lot: marss at 4 sites x 25 years has power 0.735 for a 3%/yr decline, against 0.145 for one site.
  • Plan with --vary n_sites as well as n_years.

Literature and code check
  • GSS/EGSS equations match Dennis et al. (2006) as restated by Dennis & Ponciano (2014): X_t = a + c X_{t-1} + E_t, Y_t = X_t + F_t. The module's c = 1 + b and a = -b log K give the stationary mean log K (= a/(1 - c)). EGSS is c = 1. Unlike Dennis et al. (2006), which start from the stationary distribution, the module estimates the initial state freely (the nonstationary variant; MARSS x0 at t = 0). It is stated in the methods text.
  • REML for EGSS: the differenced log counts have mean mu k_i, variance k_i sd_p^2 + 2 sd_o^2 and lag-1 covariance -sd_o^2 (Staples et al. 2004; Humbert et al. 2009). Code (egss_S, egss_negreml) matches.
  • Bayes: equilibrium log K = xbar - r0/b follows from setting the expected change r0 + b(x - xbar) to 0. Priors in code match README/methods.
  • Knape (2008): b is weakly identified when both variances are unknown, and how badly depends on the parameter values; a known observation variance removes the problem. This matches the module's text and the new parameter-dependence result below.
  • Fixed: the exponential (REML) methods text claimed a GLS trend with a t-based interval. The code reports the mean and quantiles of the grid-integrated mixture. The methods text and the reml.R header now describe what the code does.
  • The simulators share no code with fit() beyond ts_drop_years, and the independent simulators below share none at all.

What this adds to the builder's results:

  • Trends, abundance, equilibrium N and covariate effects pass at independent designs too. False-positive rates are 0.059-0.082, except REML single-series trends, which are conservative (0.000 here, 0.025 at the default).
  • Density dependence is parameter-dependent, not uniformly unrecoverable. With b = -0.5 and process noise larger than counting error, 40 years of Gompertz ML recovered b without bias (coverage 0.96). The Bayes model (U(-2, 0) prior, 30 yr) still overstated it by 33%. The builder's FAIL for b holds at its own noise ratio (sd_o > sd_p) and should not be read as "never". Without independent evidence about the noise ratio, a researcher cannot tell which regime applies, so the report's caution stays.
  • Power of REML single-series trends (limit 10). Oracle comparison (oracle_trend.R, 1000 series at the exp_v design): GLS with the true variances had coverage 0.936, power 0.595 and interval width 0.070 (log scale). Plug-in REML GLS had coverage 0.856 (invalid) and power 0.58. The module had coverage 1.000, power 0.000 and width 0.196. Validity is kept at a high price. The builder's prior study found the same trade-off at the default design. This is a design limit to report, not a bug.
  • loglinear under random-walk dynamics (ll_rw_rho.R): the fitted AR(1) rho exceeded 0.9 in 66% of series (median 0.945). Coverage was 0.89 for those and 0.69 for the rest, so the rho warning catches only some cases. The fixed caveat sentence therefore states the 0.825 coverage for every loglinear fit.

Engine, references and full details

Engine: hand-written REML / Kalman-filter maximum likelihood (exponential, Gompertz, multi-site default); MARSS package (other multi-site structures); nimble MCMC (Bayesian with covariates and missing years); glmmTMB (log-linear trend with AR(1), comparison).
