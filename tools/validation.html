Validation

How we know it works

Every module was built by one AI agent and checked by a second, independent one, against known answers.

The short version

29
modules, each built by one AI agent and verified by a second, independent agent
168
problems found by the verifiers: 1 critical, 46 major, the rest minor
120
problems fixed, including the critical one
19 + 10
worked examples marked DONE + OPEN at their own design

Every module passes the acceptance gate at its default design. Problems that were not fixed are written into each module's validation record as known limits, with numbers: for example, “at 100 sites the occupancy slope is biased +11.5%”. The agent reads those limits and warns you when your design is near one.

What “validated” means here: the method got known true values back from simulated data, at a stated design, within fixed limits. It does not mean the method is right for every data set, or that your data meet the model's assumptions. That is why every analysis repeats the check at your design.

How each module was built and checked

  1. A builder agent writes the module

    From a written specification and the primary literature, one agent writes the model, a simulator that generates data from that model, the report text and the figures. It then runs the planted-truth check at a default design and at harder designs, and records the results in the module's validation file.

  2. An independent verifier tries to prove it wrong

    A second agent, told to assume the builder's claims may be wrong, then:

    • checks the literature: reads the original papers and package documentation and compares the equations, priors and derived quantities in the code line by line;
    • writes its own simulator: builds new simulated data without reusing the builder's code, at a different design and different true values, fits 100 or more data sets and checks bias and coverage (bugs in a simulator and a model can cancel out, so the second simulator matters);
    • runs negative controls: confirms the check can fail (a deliberately wrong model must fail it) and that an effect of zero is declared significant about 5% of the time, as it should be;
    • cross-checks against established packages such as unmarked, secr, spOccupancy, lavaan and vegan, or against a second engine, on the same data;
    • tests usability: feeds in messy data (missing values, a misspelled site ID, a wrong column name) and checks that the error messages are clear, and reads every sentence of the report for correct numbers and wording.

    The verifier fixes real problems, re-runs the affected checks, and adds an “Independent verification” section to the module's validation record.

  3. A worked example is run end to end

    Each module has a realistic, simulated example study with a researcher's question and prompt. The full workflow is run on it, including planted truth at the example's own design. These are the examples on this site and in Appendix S1.

The acceptance gate, in plain words

An analysis is called DONE only when all of these hold. The thresholds are fixed in the code and are never loosened to make a case pass.

  1. Plan first

    What will be estimated, with which model and which checks, is written down before your data are fitted.

  2. Planted truth at your design

    At least 100 simulated data sets (50 if each fit takes minutes) with your design. For every quantity that will be interpreted:

    • bias: the average estimate is within 10% of the truth (or within 3 Monte Carlo standard errors, whichever is larger);
    • coverage: the 95% intervals contain the truth at least 95% of the time, minus 3 Monte Carlo standard errors (never below 80%);
    • convergence: at least 90% of the simulated fits converge.

  3. Your fit converged

    Bayesian models: R-hat below 1.01 and an effective sample size of at least 400 for every reported quantity, with trace plots inspected. Likelihood models: the optimiser converged and no estimate sits on a boundary.

  4. The model fits your data

    A goodness-of-fit check suited to the model, such as residual checks, MacKenzie-Bailey tests, posterior predictive checks or d-separation tests. A failed check is reported, not hidden.

  5. Reproducible, and limits stated

    Every number in the report comes from a script that can be re-run. Limits that apply to your design are stated in the report.

If a check fails, the analysis is OPEN

You still get the results, but marked OPEN, with the reason and what can and cannot be trusted. For example, the population time-series example answers “is the herd declining?” but not “is growth density dependent?”, because the density-dependence estimate was biased by -59% at that design.

Why independent verification matters

The one critical error

The open-population spatial capture-recapture module (scr-open) estimates abundance, survival and recruitment over several years. Its builder gave each year's entry probability its own “flat” Uniform(0, 1) prior. That looks harmless, but together those priors strongly favour the idea that almost every possible animal entered the population at some point, so estimates are pulled upward whenever the data are weak.

The builder's own checks passed. The verifier's independent simulator, at a different design, found the problem: abundance was biased upward by 16 to 20% (superpopulation +19%, interval coverage 0.86), and the effect of elevation on density was 23% too strong (-0.86 against a true -0.70). Only 10 of 23 quantities passed. A maximum-likelihood fit of the same model was close to the truth, which showed the likelihood was right and the prior was to blame.

The fix was the superpopulation parameterisation of Kéry & Schaub (2012): one parameter for the share of animals ever alive, and entry probabilities that sum to one. With it, 23 of 23 quantities passed (superpopulation +8.1%, coverage 0.95; elevation effect -9.5%, coverage 0.91).

Two lessons. Reasonable-looking choices can bias results by 20% without any error message. And a check is only as good as its independence: the builder's tests could not see a problem the builder's own assumptions created.

What DONE and OPEN mean

Every worked example, and every analysis you run, ends with one of two verdicts.

DONE: every quantity the report interprets passed the planted-truth check at that design. Estimates were close to the truth on average, the intervals contained the truth about as often as they claim, and the fits converged.

OPEN: at least one quantity the report interprets did not pass at that design, so CascadiaTools does not certify it. It does not mean the module is broken: all 29 modules pass at their default designs. And it does not mean everything is wrong: usually most quantities pass and one falls short, often narrowly (for example, an interval that contains the truth 90% of the time when it should be 95%). The module page shows exactly which quantity, by how much, and why.

If your own analysis comes back OPEN, CascadiaTools tells you what you can still use and suggests what would help: more data (more sites, surveys, animals or years), a simpler model, a different estimator, or reading that quantity with extra caution (for example, treating a 95% interval as roughly 90%).

10 of the 29 worked examples are OPEN at their example designs. We kept them that way on purpose: they show the harness reporting a limit honestly, which is the point of checking.
