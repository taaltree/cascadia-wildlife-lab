    What is mapped

    The tool covers every bird in the region with at least 1,000 June-July detections, plus the pilot species: 242 in all. Each map shows the modeled probability that one standard checklist reports the species: 60 minutes, 1 km traveled, one observer, 7 am, July 1. It is calculated on a 1 km grid (1.59 million km², Oregon, Washington, Idaho and British Columbia) for each year from 2002 to 2018. It is not occupancy, abundance, or a count of birds.

    Data

    • Records: eBird complete checklists, so checklists without the species count as non-detections. Stationary or traveling, up to 5 hours, up to 5 km, up to 10 observers, June-July, 2002-2018, with shared checklists collapsed. That leaves 269,172 checklists. Records filed under an eBird form or intergrade of a species count for that species. Until about 2016 most American Coot records were filed as a form, so leaving these out created a false jump, which our quality checks caught. Only American Coot and Red Crossbill lost over 1% of their records, so only those two were refit; for the rest the difference is at most 0.4%.
    • Withheld records: eBird withholds some sensitive species, sometimes only by state or date. Treating those gaps as non-detections would create false absences, so affected regions are left unmapped. White-headed Woodpecker and American Barn Owl are fitted and mapped for the US only, because their BC records appear to be withheld (0 detections on 10,483 and 36,756 BC checklists inside their mapped ranges, where 92 and 149 were expected).

    The models

    All models are LightGBM binary classifiers: 400 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, at least 50 checklists per leaf, 80% sampling of rows and covariates, L2 penalty 1. These settings are fixed, not tuned per species. 85% of checklists train the model. The other 15%, thinned to one checklist per 3 km cell, week and year, calibrate probabilities by Platt scaling. Apart from the experimental model, no model has a year term, so a map can change between years only through its covariates.

    Habitat area panel. The side panel's Measure menu gives three readings of the same yearly maps, for the region and model chosen: the mean probability; the expected area, the sum of the probabilities over 1 km cells in km², a smooth index in which a broad rise in low probabilities counts as much as a rise in the core; and the area above a threshold, the area of cells at or above a cutoff chosen per species and model to make the sum of the false-negative and false-positive rates smallest on held-out checklists (the maximum of sensitivity + specificity − 1). Areas are counted inside the range clip when the species is clipped. Thresholds are chosen at actual checklist effort while the maps use the standard checklist, and they are low for rare species, so the thresholded area is an index, not a measured occupied area. Neither is a population trend. Tested on 18 simulated species with known habitat change: the expected area followed the true series closely (median correlation 0.94 for the average of three climate models, false change under 0.3% when nothing changed). The thresholded area failed: about 80% of cells sit above the cutoff every year, so it barely moves (median correlation 0.69 for the same model). Use it for the size of the area, not for its change.

    Landsat model

    Landsat composites (Collection 2, June-September, Landsat 4-9 harmonized to one sensor scale; water, cloud, shadow and snow masked) were checked against MODIS (NDVI r 0.98) and across sensors (Landsat 7 vs 8 in the same year, r 0.99). The harvest detector agrees with Hansen's Global Forest Change on where forest was lost over 2001-2024 (r 0.82 per 1 km cell) but is noisier year by year, so the model uses multi-year young-stand measures, which start in 1994.

    • Simulated species whose habitat depends on young-stand quality (share of young stands and how green they regrow): the current models follow their change with correlation 0.08; with Landsat, 0.96. Map pattern 0.60 vs 0.91. Change shown where nothing changed: 1.36 times the current model, within the 1.5 limit. Weather-driven change: 0.86 vs 0.89. Passes all three tests set in advance.
    • Real species, held-out checklists (242 species, same rows as the Daymet model): prediction error 0.5% lower for new places (190 of 242 species better; young-forest species 0.7%, 14 of 15), but 0.2% higher for later years (97 of 242 better). So Landsat helps a little with where species are, especially young-forest species, and does not improve prediction of later years.
    • The simulated test used the same Landsat layers to build the truth, so it shows the model can use this information when a species depends on it, not that real species do. A Landsat-only model, which could reach back to 1985, created too much false change (1.62 times) and is not shown.

    Why it's here: what drives each map

    The side panel's "Why it's here" card shows what each model relies on. Both measures come from the same five held-out spatial folds used for the model-quality scores: each fold's model is fitted on the other four folds and examined on its own checklists, and the bands and ranges show the spread across folds.

    • Share of the map's variation. Each group of covariates (land cover, vegetation greenness, terrain and water, climate, and Landsat stand condition in the Landsat model) is shuffled among held-out checklists, keeping the group's variables together, with survey effort fixed at the standard checklist. The group's importance is how much the prediction moves (mean squared change on the logit scale); shares are importances divided by their total. Groups with many variables are not favored by construction, but a group can borrow credit from a correlated one.
    • Survey effort (duration, distance, observers, date, hour) is not habitat and is reported separately: how much held-out prediction error rises when it is shuffled. The maps hold it at the standard checklist.
    • Response curves (partial dependence) for the six most important variables: each variable is set to values across its range (2.5th to 97.5th percentile of checklists) on 2,000 held-out checklists at the standard checklist, and the predictions averaged. All curves for a species share one vertical scale, so taller curves matter more. For the average of the three climate models, climate curves average the three products at the same percentile of each, drawn on Daymet's axis.

    Test with known truth. 18 simulated species with known drivers (climate, needleleaf forest and greenness, or elevation alone) were fitted with the three climate models. Pass rules were set before the results: the top group correct for at least 14 of 18; median share error at most 0.10; curve shapes matching the truth (rank correlation at least 0.8) for at least 80% of true drivers. Results: top group correct for 16 of 18 (both misses were near-ties in the truth too); median share error 0.05; curve shapes matched for 97% of 72 drivers. Known biases: climate's share was understated by about 0.1 on average, mostly credited to land cover instead; elevation-only species kept 73-94% of the share on terrain (truth 100%), so elevation was not wrongly credited to temperature. Curve heights were about half the true effect, partly because the map shows detection at a standard checklist rather than occupancy and partly because tree models flatten curves: read the shapes and the relative heights, not the values. With real species, "drivers" are correlates of where birds are reported, which may stand in for unmeasured causes.

    Range clip

    The clip uses all extant BirdLife polygons, excluding vagrant ones, plus a 50 km buffer. Cells outside it are drawn faint: flagged, not removed. If under 95% of a species' June-July detections fall inside, the map is not clipped and says that the range map and the data disagree: 189 of 242 species are clipped. Some of the 53 unclipped species look like differences in taxonomy, where BirdLife splits what eBird treats as one species. Range maps do not feed into the models.

    Population trends

    Population trends come from a separate analysis of the North American Breeding Bird Survey, 1993-2025: a hierarchical first-difference negative binomial model with state by Bird Conservation Region strata, observer-by-route effects and a first-year effect. Definitions follow USGS (Link et al. 2020). Planted-truth recovery and comparison with USGS estimates were run before these fits, and all final fits had zero divergences. These trends measure population change and are a different quantity from the maps.

    Part of that improvement comes from averaging three fits rather than from climate. In the first 16 pilot species, compared fairly against three base fits, the gain was 2.2% (1.1 to 3.4) on new places and a tie on later years, 0.2% (−0.6 to 1.1).     Independent check against BBS counts

    The North American Breeding Bird Survey is a standardized roadside count that does not feed the maps, so it is an independent test of them. For each species and model, a Bayesian hierarchical model relates BBS counts on each route and year, 2002-2018, to the mean mapped probability within 100 m of the route path. It separates two questions:

    • Between routes: do routes with more mapped habitat have more birds?
    • Within routes: in years when the map shows more habitat along a route than that route's own average, are counts higher? This is the test of mapped change.

    Both are elasticities: 1 means counts rise in proportion to mapped habitat, 0 means no relation. The model also has route effects, each route's own trend, year effects shared by all routes (weather, detectability, regional population swings), observer-by-route effects and a first-year-observer effect, with negative binomial counts. Route paths: USGS National Atlas lines for Oregon, Washington and Idaho, and lines traced from Environment and Climate Change Canada's route maps for British Columbia; 351 of 392 routes.

    Tested first with simulated counts on real route designs (50 to 227 routes): effects recovered with bias under 0.15 and 90-100% interval coverage; false positives 0-10% when there was no effect. Two limits: on a species with very sparse counts (about 0.4 birds per route) the sampler got stuck, so fits that do not converge are not shown; and when the yearly maps are noisy versions of the habitat birds respond to, the within-route effect shrinks to about 0.35-0.5 of its true size, so within-route values here are understated.

    Loading…

    Results, 211 species. Where birds are: every model passes clearly. Routes with more mapped habitat have about proportionally more BBS birds (mean elasticity 0.92 to 0.96), with a clear positive relation for about 90% of species and a negative one for none. Change: year-to-year habitat change along a route predicts BBS change only weakly for the habitat models (about 0.1, clear for 7-14% of species). Because noisy yearly maps understate this, the true link is likely larger, but still small. Read mapped change as change in habitat conditions, not as a forecast of local counts. The experimental location + year model follows BBS change much more closely (0.46), but its year and location terms mean that is not a pure habitat signal.

    Known limits

    • Why the maps change so little. Each model learns one pooled relationship with habitat from all years. A species that is expanding or declining for reasons the covariates do not capture looks nearly the same every year. Eurasian Collared-Dove stays near 0.05 from 2002 to 2018, while the BBS index rises from 0.002 to 9.4. The experimental model below can show this kind of change, but it has not passed its test.
    • The climate and base maps do not track population trends. Across 17 species, 2002-2018 change in the average model correlates 0.20 (−0.42 to 0.74) with BBS trends. A stable map does not mean a stable population.
    • eBird yearly index is not shown. It tracks BBS (rank correlation 0.83, interval 0.57 to 0.94), but failed its test with known truth: with no real trend it reported a rise of about 0.6%/yr for species on 2% of checklists, and it reached the wrong direction in BC where trends differed by region.
    • Covariates have their own errors. Temperature products disagree on trends, and the 2015-2016 ESA land cover version change was checked and looks acceptable, but cannot be ruled out as a source of small changes. Daymet temperature shows blocky changes of several °C in British Columbia that TerraClimate and CHELSA do not (16% of BC cells change over 2 °C in maximum temperature, against none). This appears to affect the species maps little: the Daymet model's 25 km change in BC correlates about 0.09 with that temperature change (median across species, ignoring sign), and the average's change map correlates 0.94 with an average that leaves Daymet out.
    • Experimental location + year model. Shown because it is the only tested model that can show this kind of change, not because it passed. Base covariates plus grid location and year, as a mean of three fits, so year effects can differ by place. It is the only tested model that shows spread: Eurasian Collared-Dove rises from 0.001 in 2002 to 0.064 in 2018. Across all 242 species, a single fit beats one base fit by 3.3% in log loss on new places (2.9 to 3.8) and 2.8% on later years (2.3 to 3.3), and the average of three climate models by 0.8% (0.6 to 1.3) and 1.3% (1.1 to 1.7). Location may carry much of this, and the later-years test holds year at 2014, so it does not test the year effects. For the 17 species with BBS trends so far, its 2002-2018 regional trends correlate 0.73 (0.36 to 0.92) with BBS, against 0.20 for the average. It did not pass its test with known truth: real change was 2.4-2.7 times false change (limit 3), correlation for opposite regional trends was 0.39-0.44 (limit 0.7), and land-cover change was weakly recovered (0.43-0.55, against 0.82-0.85 for base). It runs about 1.8%/yr above BBS and can read changes in eBird reporting as real, so it is neither a habitat map nor a population trend.

    What is mapped

    610 birds: every species with at least 1,000 June-July detections on complete eBird checklists in 2002-2020, spread over at least 10 separate 50 km blocks. Each map shows the modeled probability that one standard checklist (60 minutes, 1 km, one observer, 7 am, early July) reports the species, in 3 km cells across the United States (without Hawaii), Canada and Mexico. The western Aleutians, which cross 180 degrees, are left out.

    Data and withheld records

    • Records: 9.1 million complete June-July checklists, 2002-2025, with the pilot's filters (stationary or traveling, up to 5 hours and 5 km, up to 10 observers; shared checklists counted once). Early years are thin: about 25,000 checklists in 2002 against 1.1 million in 2024.
    • Withheld records: eBird hides some sensitive species in some states, so their absence there is not a real absence. A state is left out of a species' training, and drawn faint and marked "not mapped", when the species is on eBird's Sensitive Species list for that state with dates overlapping June-July (list as of May 2026): 92 species-state pairs in 21 species. Check: of 60 listed pairs inside the species' mapped breeding range, 54 have no detections at all and 6 have 1 to 13, where hundreds would be expected. An earlier screen based on zero detections alone removed natural absences too and was replaced.

    AlphaEarth era: reliability

    AlphaEarth embeddings summarize each year of satellite imagery as 64 numbers per location. They cover 2017 onward, so they are kept in their own model and years rather than mixed into the 2002-2018 series. Two tests with simulated species asked whether they show where species are, whether they follow real change, and whether they invent change.

    • Pilot region, 2017-2020 (18 species, truth from land cover, vegetation, climate or terrain): embeddings + Daymet passed: pattern 0.89 (base + Daymet 0.92); false change 1.4 times that of base + Daymet (limit 1.5); real-change correlation 0.76 (0.82). Embeddings alone failed: they barely follow weather-driven change (0.44).

    Why it fails. Each year's embeddings are computed afresh from that year's imagery. Differences in cloud cover, the number and timing of images, the satellites available and the embedding model itself shift the 64 numbers even where the land has not changed. The species model reads those shifts as changes in habitat. Over large regions they cancel, which is why the regional mean does not drift; in any one place they do not.

    What to use. The AlphaEarth maps show where species are about as well as the other models, and regional series are sound. The multi-year change views (2017-2020 against 2021-2024) are shown as requested, labeled as failing this test: read only large, consistent patterns, and confirm them with the 2002-2018 models or other data. Year-to-year change in this model is mostly noise. Embeddings cannot say why a species changed. Not yet tested: averaging embeddings over two or three years before fitting, which may reduce the noise.

    Known limits

    • Uneven data. eBird coverage is far denser in the United States and southern Canada than in the north and in Mexico, and far denser after 2010 than before. Maps are least reliable where checklists are scarce: held-out scores in Mexico are clearly lower (above).
    • Habitat change is not population trend. Change views show change in modeled habitat conditions. On the pilot, independent BBS counts confirmed where birds are (elasticity about 0.94) but tracked year-to-year habitat change only weakly.
    • Climate after 2024. Daymet ends in 2024, so the AlphaEarth era stops there. MODIS vegetation is missing for 2024 in part of the continent; the AlphaEarth model does not use it.

    What the scores mean

    AUC
    The chance a checklist that reported the species is ranked above one that did not. 0.5 is no skill, 1 is perfect. It looks good for rare species almost regardless, so read it with the next two.
    PR-AUC and PR-AUC ÷ prevalence
    How precisely the model finds detections. Its baseline is the share of checklists reporting the species, so the ratio shows skill above that baseline: 1 is no skill.
    Deviance explained
    Improvement in log loss over predicting one constant, the held-out reporting rate. 0 is no better than that constant and below 0 is worse. That constant uses the test data's own rate, so this score is slightly conservative.
    Prediction ÷ observed
    Mean predicted probability over the observed reporting rate. 1 means no overall bias, below 1 means predictions run low.
    Calibration chart
    Checklists grouped by predicted probability, with the share in each group that reported the species. Points on the diagonal mean a predicted 10% happened about 10% of the time. Groups with under 50 checklists are left out.

    Scores use actual checklist effort, while maps are drawn at a standard checklist. Base and location + year are scored as single fits, though their maps average three, so they slightly understate the mapped models. Scores are blank when a species has under 10 held-out detections in that region and test. Download all scores (CSV), shared under the same eBird Terms of Use.

    How to read these maps. They show how suitable conditions were, learned from all years together. They do not show where a species occurred in a given year, so a colonizing species can appear years before it arrived. The experimental location + year model works differently and carries its own warning. Values are relative probabilities at one 60-minute, 1 km morning checklist on July 1, not occupancy or abundance. Maps do not capture changes unrelated to the covariates. A stable map does not mean a stable population. In tests with known truth, real change was about 2-3 times the false change at 1 km and about 4.5-7 times at 25 km, under favorable conditions: read small differences at 1 km with caution.
