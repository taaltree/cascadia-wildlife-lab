    What is mapped

    The tool covers every bird in the region with at least 1,000 June-July detections, plus the pilot species: 242 in all. Each map shows the modeled probability that one standard checklist reports the species: 60 minutes, 1 km traveled, one observer, 7 am, July 1. It is calculated on a 1 km grid (1.59 million km², Oregon, Washington, Idaho and British Columbia) for each year from 2002 to 2018. It is not occupancy, abundance, or a count of birds.

    Data

    • Records: eBird complete checklists, so checklists without the species count as non-detections. Stationary or traveling, up to 5 hours, up to 5 km, up to 10 observers, June-July, 2002-2018, with shared checklists collapsed. That leaves 269,172 checklists. Records filed under an eBird form or intergrade of a species count for that species. Until about 2016 most American Coot records were filed as a form, so leaving these out created a false jump, which our quality checks caught. Only American Coot and Red Crossbill lost over 1% of their records, so only those two were refit; for the rest the difference is at most 0.4%.
    • Withheld records: eBird withholds some sensitive species, sometimes only by state or date. Treating those gaps as non-detections would create false absences, so affected regions are left unmapped. White-headed Woodpecker and American Barn Owl are fitted and mapped for the US only, because their BC records appear to be withheld (0 detections on 10,483 and 36,756 BC checklists inside their mapped ranges, where 92 and 149 were expected).

    The models

    All models are LightGBM binary classifiers: 400 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, at least 50 checklists per leaf, 80% sampling of rows and covariates, L2 penalty 1. These settings are fixed, not tuned per species. 85% of checklists train the model. The other 15%, thinned to one checklist per 3 km cell, week and year, calibrate probabilities by Platt scaling. Apart from the experimental model, no model has a year term, so a map can change between years only through its covariates.

    Change views

    • Each year: the model applied to that year's covariates.
    • 5-year running mean: the mean of a year and the two on each side.
    • Change: the 2014-2018 mean minus the 2002-2006 mean.
    • Yearly trend: the linear slope across 2002-2018, in probability per year.
    • 25 km views: the mean over 25 km blocks.

    Range clip

    The clip uses all extant BirdLife polygons, excluding vagrant ones, plus a 50 km buffer. Cells outside it are drawn faint: flagged, not removed. If under 95% of a species' June-July detections fall inside, the map is not clipped and says that the range map and the data disagree: 189 of 242 species are clipped. Some of the 53 unclipped species look like differences in taxonomy, where BirdLife splits what eBird treats as one species. Range maps do not feed into the models.

    Population trends

    Population trends come from a separate analysis of the North American Breeding Bird Survey, 1993-2025: a hierarchical first-difference negative binomial model with state by Bird Conservation Region strata, observer-by-route effects and a first-year effect. Definitions follow USGS (Link et al. 2020). Planted-truth recovery and comparison with USGS estimates were run before these fits, and all final fits had zero divergences. These trends measure population change and are a different quantity from the maps.

    Part of that improvement comes from averaging three fits rather than from climate. In the first 16 pilot species, compared fairly against three base fits, the gain was 2.2% (1.1 to 3.4) on new places and a tie on later years, 0.2% (−0.6 to 1.1). So climate helps to map new places but has not been shown to help predict later years. Probabilities run low, especially for rare species, so read them as relative. These tests say nothing about whether change over time is right: that is what the tests with known truth and the BBS comparison are for.

    Known limits

    • Why the maps change so little. Each model learns one pooled relationship with habitat from all years. A species that is expanding or declining for reasons the covariates do not capture looks nearly the same every year. Eurasian Collared-Dove stays near 0.05 from 2002 to 2018, while the BBS index rises from 0.002 to 9.4. The experimental model below can show this kind of change, but it has not passed its test.
    • The climate and base maps do not track population trends. Across 17 species, 2002-2018 change in the average model correlates 0.20 (−0.42 to 0.74) with BBS trends. A stable map does not mean a stable population.
    • eBird yearly index is not shown. It tracks BBS (rank correlation 0.83, interval 0.57 to 0.94), but failed its test with known truth: with no real trend it reported a rise of about 0.6%/yr for species on 2% of checklists, and it reached the wrong direction in BC where trends differed by region.
    • Covariates have their own errors. Temperature products disagree on trends, and the 2015-2016 ESA land cover version change was checked and looks acceptable, but cannot be ruled out as a source of small changes. Daymet temperature shows blocky changes of several °C in British Columbia that TerraClimate and CHELSA do not (16% of BC cells change over 2 °C in maximum temperature, against none). This appears to affect the species maps little: the Daymet model's 25 km change in BC correlates about 0.09 with that temperature change (median across species, ignoring sign), and the average's change map correlates 0.94 with an average that leaves Daymet out.
    • Experimental location + year model. Shown because it is the only tested model that can show this kind of change, not because it passed. Base covariates plus grid location and year, as a mean of three fits, so year effects can differ by place. It is the only tested model that shows spread: Eurasian Collared-Dove rises from 0.001 in 2002 to 0.064 in 2018. Across all 242 species, a single fit beats one base fit by 3.3% in log loss on new places (2.9 to 3.8) and 2.8% on later years (2.3 to 3.3), and the average of three climate models by 0.8% (0.6 to 1.3) and 1.3% (1.1 to 1.7). Location may carry much of this, and the later-years test holds year at 2014, so it does not test the year effects. For the 17 species with BBS trends so far, its 2002-2018 regional trends correlate 0.73 (0.36 to 0.92) with BBS, against 0.20 for the average. It did not pass its test with known truth: real change was 2.4-2.7 times false change (limit 3), correlation for opposite regional trends was 0.39-0.44 (limit 0.7), and land-cover change was weakly recovered (0.43-0.55, against 0.82-0.85 for base). It runs about 1.8%/yr above BBS and can read changes in eBird reporting as real, so it is neither a habitat map nor a population trend.

    • Season and area. The maps cover the June-July breeding season in the pilot region only.

    What the scores mean

    AUC
    The chance a checklist that reported the species is ranked above one that did not. 0.5 is no skill, 1 is perfect. It looks good for rare species almost regardless, so read it with the next two.
    PR-AUC and PR-AUC ÷ prevalence
    How precisely the model finds detections. Its baseline is the share of checklists reporting the species, so the ratio shows skill above that baseline: 1 is no skill.
    Deviance explained
    Improvement in log loss over predicting one constant, the held-out reporting rate. 0 is no better than that constant and below 0 is worse. That constant uses the test data's own rate, so this score is slightly conservative.
    Prediction ÷ observed
    Mean predicted probability over the observed reporting rate. 1 means no overall bias, below 1 means predictions run low.
    Calibration chart
    Checklists grouped by predicted probability, with the share in each group that reported the species. Points on the diagonal mean a predicted 10% happened about 10% of the time. Groups with under 50 checklists are left out.

    Scores use actual checklist effort, while maps are drawn at a standard checklist. Base and location + year are scored as single fits, though their maps average three, so they slightly understate the mapped models. Scores are blank when a species has under 10 held-out detections in that region and test. Download all scores (CSV), shared under the same eBird Terms of Use.

    How to read these maps. They show how suitable conditions were, learned from all years together. They do not show where a species occurred in a given year, so a colonizing species can appear years before it arrived. The experimental location + year model works differently and carries its own warning. Values are relative probabilities at one 60-minute, 1 km morning checklist on July 1, not occupancy or abundance. Maps do not capture changes unrelated to the covariates. A stable map does not mean a stable population. In tests with known truth, real change was about 2-3 times the false change at 1 km and about 4.5-7 times at 25 km, under favorable conditions: read small differences at 1 km with caution.
